# Chapter 3: Linear Map

## 1. What problem does this chapter aim to solve?

**Core mission:** The core mission is to develop linear maps from a definition into a complete structural theory—centered on the dimension formula—and to provide the tool that turns them into computable objects: matrices.
## 2. Chapter Roadmap

### First Leap (Linear Map)

- Introduce the protagonist of this book:**linear maps**. Define linear maps and the operations above, and naturally conclude through analysis that linear maps themselves also form a vector space.

### Second Leap (Null Space And Range)

- In the previous section, we obtained that the set of linear maps is also a vector space. What is an important element in this space? It is the zero element. The zero linear map is special. We know that the zero map sends every vector to $0$. Explicitly, $\{0\}$ is a subspace. There is always a subspace that maps to $0$—namely, the null space (kernel)—and on this subspace the linear map is zero. Now let us turn our attention to the entire range. The map sends $V \setminus \ker T$ to $\operatorname{range}(T) \setminus \{0\}$. The range is also a subspace of $W$. Is $V \setminus \ker T$ a subspace? Clearly not. But we can find a subspace $U$ such that $V = U \oplus \ker T$. Does $U$ map onto the range? Yes. Therefore, $\dim(\operatorname{range}(T)) + \dim(\ker T) = \dim(V)$.

### Third Leap (Matrix)

- Above, we have always introduced linear maps in a conceptual, vague way. I mean, can we find a distinct and tidy way of sending an arbitrary $v \in V$ to $W$? Let us turn our attention to the list of equations from the last section; by it we can turn $F^n$ into $F^m$. How do we find a map between two vector spaces? Let $T v_k = w_k$, where $\{v_k\}$ is a basis of $V$ and $w_k \in W$. Now we select a basis of $W$. Let the coordinates of the $w_k$ with respect to this basis define the matrix of the linear map. Explicitly, this matrix exists and is unique. Now a matrix can express a linear map between two specific bases, and addition and scalar multiplication of linear maps correspond to addition and scalar multiplication of matrices. A question is how to represent the **composition of maps** by matrices: it is **matrix multiplication**. This section introduces row rank. The interesting point is to prove that row rank equals column rank. A matrix $A$ can be factorized as $A = C R$, where $C$ is full column rank and $R$ is full row rank, and their ranks equal the column rank or row rank of $A$. If $C$ is full column rank, then the row vectors of $A$ are linear combinations of the rows of $R$. Hence, the row rank of $A$ is at most the column rank of $A$. Applying the same argument to $A^t$, we also get the reverse inequality. Therefore, row rank equals column rank. Here, why is the matrix only restricted by $\operatorname{range} T$? Because for an arbitrary choice of basis, the column space of the matrix is just the coordinate representation of $\operatorname{range} T$, so its dimension is $\dim(\operatorname{range} T)$, independent of the choice of basis.

### Fourth Leap(Invertibility And Isomorphisms)

- We know that a map $T$ could have an inverse $T^{-1}$ such that $T T^{-1}=1$ and $T^{-1}T=1$. In particular, among linear maps, invertible maps exist, such as the identity map $1$ in $L(V)$. If an inverse exists, it is unique. A map is invertible if and only if it is injective and surjective.
- We know $V=V$, which is trivial. What I mean is: for an arbitrary finite-dimensional vector space, does there exist another vector space that differs only in the names of its elements? For example, $\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, or $\mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{C}$ with addition and scalar multiplication.
- If two vector spaces are connected by an invertible linear map, this tells us that their structures are the same; they are isomorphic. The map and the matrix are not themselves isomorphic; rather, the space of linear maps and the space of matrices are isomorphic after choosing bases. In the last section, we found the matrix of a linear map from two bases in linear algebra. Isomorphism shows that operations and elements can be mapped between them:
- $Tv \leftrightarrow \mathcal{M}(T)\mathcal{M}(v)$,$T+T' \leftrightarrow \mathcal{M}(T)+\mathcal{M}(T')$,$\lambda T \leftrightarrow \lambda \mathcal{M}(T)$,$ST \leftrightarrow \mathcal{M}(S)\mathcal{M}(T)$.
- We should know that matrices are created to represent maps with respect to bases. By matrices, we can also see dimension through rank; more precisely, the rank gives the dimension of the image. What about the inverse? If $T$ is invertible, then $\mathcal{M}(T)$ is invertible, and $\mathcal{M}(T^{-1})=\mathcal{M}(T)^{-1}$. In the book, the notation is so neat that$\mathcal{M}(ST,(u_1,\ldots,u_m),(w_1,\ldots,w_p))=\mathcal{M}(S,(v_1,\ldots,v_n),(w_1,\ldots,w_p))\mathcal{M}(T,(u_1,\ldots,u_m),(v_1,\ldots,v_n)).$
### Fivth Leap(Product And Quotients)

- Explicitly, the field $F$ can be used to form $F^n$ by taking$F \times F \times \cdots \times F$ ($n$ times).Can an arbitrary vector space $V$ be treated in this way? Yes. Its $n$-fold product is$V^n = V \times V \times \cdots \times V$,whose elements are $(v_1,\ldots,v_n)$.Its dimension is$\dim(V^n) = n\dim(V)$. We connect the product and the direct sum. Why? For finitely many spaces, the direct product and the external direct sum are essentially the same construction. For subspaces $U$ and $W$ of a vector space $V$, the map $U \times W \to V,\quad (u,w)\mapsto u+w$ is injective if and only if $U \cap W = \{0\}$. It is surjective if and only if $U+W=V$. Hence it is an isomorphism onto $V$ exactly when $V = U \oplus W$. In that case, a basis of the direct sum is obtained by taking the union of a basis of $U$ and a basis of $W$, with the two bases treated as belonging to different summands. Thus, in this sense, direct sum and product correspond.

- Now we want to decompose a subspace. We know how to describe the null space $\operatorname{null}T$ and the range $\operatorname{range}T$. But $\operatorname{range}T$ is a subspace of the codomain $W$, while $\operatorname{null}T$ is a subspace of the domain $V$; they are not simply two subspaces of the same space unless $V=W$ and extra conditions hold. Also, $\operatorname{range}T$ itself is a subspace, not a non-subspace. What is not a subspace is, for example, an arbitrary complement of $\operatorname{range}T$ in $W$; one must choose a complement.

- Then the section defines the quotient vector space $V/U$. Its elements are the cosets$\{v+U\}$,and its dimension is$\dim(V/U)=\dim(V)-\dim(U)$,provided $V$ is finite-dimensional and $U$ is a subspace of $V$.
### Sixth Leap(Linear Functional And Dual Space)

- A linear functional on $V$ is a linear map from $V$ to $F$, and such maps are special. All linear functionals on $V$ form a subspace called the dual space $V'$. Given a basis $\{v_1,\ldots,v_n\}$ of $V$, one obtains a dual basis $\{f_1,\ldots,f_n\}$ of $V'$ such that $f_k(v_j)=\delta_{kj}$, that is, $f_k(v_k)=1$ and $f_k(v_j)=0$ for $j\neq k$. Now we can find the coordinates of a vector in $V$ with respect to a specific basis: if $v=\sum_{j=1}^n a_j v_j$, then $a_k=f_k(v)$. If $T:V\to W$, finding the coordinates of $T(v)$ in a given basis of $W$ is different from finding the coordinates of $v$ in a given basis of $V$; it requires the matrix of $T$. For $f\in W'$, we define the dual map $T':W'\to V'$ by $T'(f)=f\circ T$, so $(T'(f))(v)=f(T(v))$ for all $v\in V$.
- The annihilator of a subspace $U$ is the set of linear functionals that vanish on $U$; this set forms a subspace. Its dimension can be computed in finite dimensions: if $U\subseteq V$, then $\dim U^0=\dim V-\dim U$. It is enough to define bases of $V$ and $W$, and the corresponding dual bases of $V'$ and $W'$. For the null space of $T'$, it equals the annihilator of $\operatorname{range}T$, so its dimension is determined: if $V$ and $W$ are finite-dimensional, then $\dim(\operatorname{null}T')=\dim W-\operatorname{rank}T$. Here $T'$ is a map from $W'$ to $V'$, and $\operatorname{null}T'$ consists of all linear functionals on $W$ that vanish on $\operatorname{range}T$. Moreover, with respect to dual bases, $\mathcal{M}(T')=\mathcal{M}(T)^t$.
## 3. Summary

> 这个章节叙述主要叙述了线性映射本身的结构，通过观察它的作用，core是零空间和rangeT。并给出可计算的工具——矩阵。对于商空间，通过对零空间的分离，构造一个与rangeT的同构，更能看清映射结构。
> The core role of the dual space is to shift linear algebra from looking only at vectors themselves to looking at all linear functionals that measure vectors: the dual space $V'=\mathcal{L}(V,F)$ consists of linear rulers, and once a basis $\{v_1,\dots,v_n\}$ of $V$ is chosen, the dual basis $\{f_1,\dots,f_n\}$ satisfies $f_k(v_j)=\delta_{kj}$, so for $v=\sum_{j=1}^n a_jv_j$ the coordinates are $a_k=f_k(v)$, meaning that coordinates are fundamentally measurement results; for a linear map $T:V\to W$, the dual map $T':W'\to V'$ defined by $T'(f)=f\circ T$ pulls rulers on $W$ back to rulers on $V$, reversing the direction, and thereby translates the image and kernel of $T$ into annihilators: $\operatorname{null}T'=(\operatorname{range}T)^0$ and $\operatorname{range}T'=(\operatorname{null}T)^0$, where $U^0=\{f\in V'\mid f(u)=0,\ \forall u\in U\}$; in finite dimensions $\dim U^0=\dim V-\dim U$, hence $\dim(\operatorname{null}T')=\dim W-\operatorname{rank}T$, and with respect to dual bases $\mathcal{M}(T')=\mathcal{M}(T)^t$, so matrix transpose is not an accident but the algebraic expression of pulling measurements back; thus the dual space does not primarily make computation easier but provides a symmetric measurement-space perspective that unifies coordinates, image, kernel, annihilators, transpose, and dimension relations, and prepares for orthogonal complements, Riesz representation, and adjoint operators in inner product spaces; unlike the quotient space, which works internally by killing the kernel and decomposing a map as surjection-isomorphism-injection, the dual space works externally by reflecting structure through measurement, and both serve the same goal of understanding the structure of linear maps.
